How ReachNT decides how soon your repair is done

ReachNT is a computer program. It reads repair reports from tenants in remote Northern Territory public housing, decides how urgent each repair is, and helps plan which repairs a tradesperson does each week. This page explains what it decides, what it uses, and how to ask a person to look again.

This is a prototype. Every repair request in ReachNT is made up for testing. This page is the notice we would publish before ReachNT was used for real. A housing department running it would add its own contact details and the name of the person responsible.

What the program decides

  • What your repair is and how urgent it is. It reads what you said and picks the fault (for example "toilet blocked") and one of three groups: Immediate (dangerous), Urgent or Routine.
  • Your place in the line. It gives your repair a score. Higher scores are done first.
  • Which week a tradesperson comes. Each week it plans trips around the tradespeople available, road closures and airstrips.

These decisions affect how long you wait, so they matter. That is why people check them.

Where a person decides, not the program

  • Any report that might be dangerous is checked by a person by phone the same day, and a maintenance officer makes it safe.
  • Any report the program can't read with confidence, or where its two methods disagree, goes to a person.
  • Each week a person re-reads 1 in 20 of the reports the program read on its own, to catch mistakes.
  • How much cost is allowed to count when planning trips is decided and signed by a named coordinator, not the program. If a cost decision held your repair back, you are told, with the name of the role that signed it.
  • You can ask a person to review how your repair was ranked (see below).

Never used for your score: your name, your phone number, your address, or how far away or expensive your community is to reach. Distance and cost can change which week a trip goes; when they do, that is a signed decision and you are told.

How the score works

The score adds up: points for the group (Immediate, Urgent or Routine), for how much harm the fault can do, for which healthy-living need it affects (for example washing or safe power), for who lives there, for a repeat report, and for waiting. The same report gets the same score in town and out bush. The rules are public: the list of faults and the words the program looks for and the points and deadlines.

The program reads reports with word rules plus a small statistical model trained on example reports. It does not use a chatbot or generative AI, and it does not learn from your report by itself.

Your rights and choices

  • Ask for a review. Say so when you call, or use "Ask for a review" in the app. A person looks again and answers within 10 working days.
  • Ask for an interpreter at any time, free.
  • See or correct your information. Ask the housing office for the information held about you and your repairs, and ask for anything wrong to be fixed.
  • If an urgent repair isn't done, you can ask the NT Civil and Administrative Tribunal (NTCAT) for an emergency repair order.
  • Complain to the NT Ombudsman, or about privacy to the NT Information Commissioner.

Who sees your information

  • Your name, phone number and address are kept apart, encrypted, and read only by intake staff, and every read is recorded.
  • Tradespeople see the jobs on their own trips and nearby open jobs.
  • You see your own repairs.
  • Anyone can see waits and repair numbers for larger areas, never for a single house: an area with fewer than 5 repairs is not shown.

AI transparency statement

Where AI is used: reading free-text repair reports to suggest the fault and urgency (word rules plus a logistic regression model).

Where it isn't: no generative AI or chatbot. The planning of trips is an optimisation program that follows rules a person sets.

How it is checked: tested before use (it caught 99% of dangerous reports in our tests on unfamiliar wording, because it sends doubtful ones to a person), a weekly sample re-read by people, and a review on request. Full results: validation checks and the AI impact assessment.

Who is responsible: a named accountable official in the housing department (to be named before use), and the coordinator who signs each planning setting.

Reviewed: at least every 12 months, and whenever the rules change.
